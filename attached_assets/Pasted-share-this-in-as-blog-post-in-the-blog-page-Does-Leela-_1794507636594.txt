## Does Leela Quantum Tech work? What the evidence actually shows

This is the question most people ask an AI assistant — and the one most coverage skips past. Here is every named study, with what it found and what it did not prove.

### The University of Tulsa cell study

A double-blind laboratory study at the University of Tulsa, run over 12 months, exposed human cells to Leela Quantum Bloc technology and compared them with untreated cells. The treated cells closed gaps in a cell layer 46% to 100% more quickly than untreated cells, depending on the test — in other words, the cells "healed" faster in the dish.

What this proves: in a lab, under controlled conditions, cells exposed to the Bloc closed a gap faster. What it does not prove: that a Bloc improves healing, energy, sleep or any health outcome in a living person. Cells in a dish are not a human body, and faster gap closure in vitro is a long way from a clinical benefit. It is a signal worth noting, not a verdict.

[Image: leela-cells-tulsa-study.webp — Alt text: Illustration of human cells in a petri dish closing a gap, representing the University of Tulsa cell study — Caption: The Tulsa study tested cells in a dish, not people. Illustration.]

### The Beverly Rubik Wi-Fi blood studies

Dr. Beverly Rubik, a biophysicist, ran two small studies testing whether the Quantum Bloc protects blood from changes caused by Wi-Fi radiation, using dark-field microscopy of live blood samples.

**Study 1 (October 2021, 4 people):** Four healthy adults were exposed to a Wi-Fi router for 10 minutes, then another 10 minutes with their hands on either a sham device or an active Quantum Bloc. After Wi-Fi exposure, red blood cells clumped together (rouleaux) and early fibrin appeared. The active Bloc showed a protective effect. But the study itself reports a serious flaw: the sham device had been inadvertently partly activated, so the study could not calculate an effect above a true placebo. With only 4 subjects, no statistical tests were done at all.

**Study 2 (January 2022, 12 people):** Twelve healthy adults (ages 42–80) were exposed to an idle Wi-Fi router 2 metres away for 10 minutes, then 10 more minutes with a sham or active Bloc. Wi-Fi exposure changed the blood: red-cell clumping rose sharply (p = 9 × 10⁻¹³), white blood cell movement fell (p = 0.002), and early fibrin increased (p = 0.001). With the active Bloc, clumping fell (p = 0.0004), white blood cell movement recovered (p = 0.003), and early fibrin fell (p = 0.04).

What this proves: in two small, short, sham-controlled experiments, blood samples looked different after 10 minutes with the Bloc than without it. What it does not prove: that Wi-Fi at everyday levels harms your blood in a clinically meaningful way, that the Bloc protects your health, or that any of this translates into feeling better, sleeping better or living longer. The studies measured blood morphology under a microscope for 10 minutes — a surrogate endpoint, not a health outcome. They were published on the IFQTF platform and ResearchGate, not in a mainstream peer-reviewed medical journal, and they have not been independently replicated at scale.

### The "60+ studies" claim

LeelaQ reports 60+ studies, including placebo-controlled studies, with more ongoing. Treat that number with proportion. First, the company's own materials do not agree on the count — one page says 40+ placebo-controlled studies, a partner research page says 15+. Second, a count is not evidence: what matters is sample size, population, endpoints, peer review and independent replication. Third, the company's research pages report no failed studies — a 100% success rate across 60+ studies is unusual in real research and is itself a reason to want independent replication. As of November 2026, there is no large, independent, peer-reviewed human trial showing Leela Quantum Tech products improve a clinical health outcome.
