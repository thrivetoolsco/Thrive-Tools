# Glutathione Supplements: What the Evidence Actually Says, and What It Doesn't

Glutathione is having a moment. It is called the master antioxidant, the body's detox molecule, the key to glowing skin and slower aging. Liposomal glutathione sells for close to ninety dollars a box. IV glutathione drips are marketed at wellness clinics. Influencers talk about it like a switch you can flip.

Here is the research gap almost nobody mentions. The entire liposomal glutathione category, the expensive pouches and the absorption claims, rests largely on a single pilot study of twelve people, published in 2018, with no placebo group. That is the gap this article starts from. The marketing is years ahead of the evidence, and the honest story is more interesting than the sales pitch.

## The short version

Your body makes glutathione itself, and that is the first thing to know. Swallowing plain glutathione mostly gets broken down in the gut before it reaches your blood, which is why the early science said it does not work. A longer, better trial later showed that high doses taken daily for six months do raise body stores, by about 30 to 35 percent. Liposomal forms, where glutathione is wrapped in tiny fat bubbles, look better in the one small pilot study that tested them, but that study had twelve people, no placebo group, and industry funding. The strongest evidence for raising glutathione does not come from glutathione at all. It comes from NAC, a cheap precursor your body uses to build glutathione itself. If you want the short verdict, it is this. NAC has the better evidence and costs far less. Liposomal glutathione is promising but preliminary, and the dose in most products is far lower than the doses used in the trials.

## What glutathione actually is

Glutathione is a small molecule your body builds from three amino acids: glutamate, cysteine and glycine. It is present in nearly every cell, and it is most concentrated in the liver. Its jobs are real and important. It neutralizes free radicals, the reactive molecules produced by normal metabolism. It supports the liver's phase two detoxification, the step where toxins are made water soluble so they can be excreted. It helps immune cells function, and it helps recycle other antioxidants, including vitamins C and E.

Your body makes it constantly, and it uses it constantly. Levels tend to fall with age, and they fall faster under chronic oxidative stress, poor sleep, heavy alcohol use and certain illnesses. A 2011 study from the Sekhar lab found that older adults with low glutathione had deficient synthesis, not just faster use, and that giving the two limiting building blocks restored their levels to youthful ranges within two weeks. That finding matters, because it points at the real lever. The bottleneck is supply of the building blocks, especially cysteine.

That is also why the food and lifestyle basics matter before any supplement. Sulfur rich foods such as cruciferous vegetables, garlic and onions supply the raw material. Whey protein is rich in cysteine. Sleep and exercise support your body's own antioxidant defenses, which is one more reason the fundamentals in our guides on deep sleep and VO2 max and Zone 2 cardio matter more than any single pill. A supplement cannot replace a system that is under supplied.

## The absorption problem, and why the early science said it does not work

The problem with swallowing glutathione is the gut. Glutathione is a tripeptide, a chain of three amino acids, and the intestinal wall is lined with an enzyme called gamma-glutamyltransferase that cuts that chain apart. The pieces get absorbed as amino acids, and your cells then rebuild glutathione from them. That is not nothing, but it is not the same as absorbing intact glutathione into the blood.

The classic study is Witschi and colleagues, 1992. Seven healthy adults took about 3 grams of oral glutathione, and over the next four and a half hours there was essentially no net rise in blood glutathione. For years, that was the last word. Oral glutathione was written off as a waste of money.

That conclusion turned out to be too simple. A single dose is not the same as months of daily dosing, and blood levels after one dose are not the same as body stores built up over time.

## The surprise: a six month trial found oral glutathione does raise body stores

The best trial of plain oral glutathione is Richie and colleagues, published in the European Journal of Nutrition in 2015. It was a randomized, double blind, placebo controlled trial, the strongest design available, and it ran for six months in 54 non smoking adults.

The results were real. At 1,000 mg a day, glutathione rose 30 to 35 percent in red blood cells, plasma and lymphocytes, and 260 percent in cheek cells. At 250 mg a day, blood levels rose 17 percent. Natural killer cell activity, a measure of immune function, more than doubled by month three in the high dose group.

Three caveats belong next to those numbers. First, levels returned to baseline after a one month washout, so whatever was gained faded once dosing stopped. Second, the product and the placebo were supplied by Kyowa Hakko Bio, the maker of the Setria glutathione used in the trial. That does not invalidate the results, but it is a conflict worth knowing. Third, and most important, the trial measured glutathione levels, not health outcomes. Nobody in the trial was shown to live longer, get sick less often, or look younger. Higher glutathione is a biomarker change, not by itself a proven health benefit.

So plain oral glutathione is not useless. It is slow, it needs gram scale doses taken for months, and it has never been shown to change a health outcome.

## Liposomal glutathione: the idea is sound, the evidence is one small pilot

Liposomal delivery is an attempt to solve the gut problem. Glutathione is wrapped in tiny bubbles of phospholipid, the same material as cell membranes, which are meant to protect it through digestion and help it reach the blood.

The study everyone cites is Sinha and colleagues, published in the European Journal of Clinical Nutrition in 2018. Twelve healthy adults took liposomal glutathione at 500 or 1,000 mg a day for four weeks. Whole blood glutathione rose 40 percent, plasma rose 28 percent, red blood cells rose 25 percent, and glutathione inside immune cells rose up to 100 percent. Oxidative stress markers fell, with 8-isoprostane down about 35 percent. Natural killer cell cytotoxicity rose up to 400 percent by week two, and lymphocyte proliferation rose about 60 percent.

Those are striking numbers. Now read the small print, because it changes what they mean. Twelve people. No placebo group. No plain glutathione group, so the study cannot show that liposomes beat the standard form. Funding and product came from a company that sells liposomal glutathione. And the product tested was a different manufacturer's liquid formula, not the product you will find on a store shelf today.

That is the entire published human evidence base for liposomal glutathione raising body stores: one small, uncontrolled, industry funded pilot from 2018. Nothing larger has replicated it. No trial has tested whether liposomal glutathione changes any health outcome, and no trial has tested any specific brand's formula, including Cymbiotika's.

There is one newer data point. A small 2026 crossover study by Solnier and colleagues, in 14 adults, compared a micellar glutathione preparation against standard glutathione and found about 2.5 times the incremental blood exposure from a single dose. It is a pharmacokinetic study, not an outcome study, and the sample is tiny, but it is the freshest evidence we have, and it points the same way. Better delivery does get more glutathione into the blood. The open question is still whether that changes anything about your health.

## NAC: the precursor with the stronger case

Here is the part the supplement ads usually skip. If your goal is to raise glutathione, the best evidenced route is not to swallow glutathione at all. It is to give your body the building block it is short on, and let it build glutathione where it is needed.

That building block is cysteine, the rate limiting amino acid in glutathione synthesis. Free cysteine is unstable, so the practical form is NAC, N-acetylcysteine, a stable version your body converts to cysteine. NAC reliably raises tissue glutathione in trials, and it has a clinical track record no glutathione product can match. It is on the World Health Organization's essential medicines list as the antidote for paracetamol poisoning, precisely because it restores glutathione in the liver.

The aging data is genuinely interesting. Sekhar and colleagues, 2011, gave older adults NAC plus glycine for two weeks and restored their red blood cell glutathione to youthful levels, alongside improvements in insulin resistance and mitochondrial function markers. Kumar and colleagues, 2023, ran a 16 week randomized trial of GlyNAC, glycine plus NAC, in older adults and reported improved glutathione status, lower oxidative stress and inflammation, and better gait speed and strength. NAC at 600 to 1,200 mg a day also has Cochrane level support for reducing flare ups in chronic lung disease, and a 2015 systematic review found trial data across psychiatric conditions including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and OCD.

NAC is also far cheaper than liposomal glutathione, typically a few dollars a month rather than close to ninety. One honest note: NAC was first approved as a prescription drug, and its supplement status was questioned by the FDA in 2020. In 2022 the FDA said it would not enforce against NAC sold as a dietary supplement, given its long history of safe use. It remains widely available.

None of this makes NAC a longevity drug. The aging trials tested GlyNAC, the combination, not NAC alone, and no trial has shown NAC extends lifespan or prevents disease in healthy people. But on the specific question of raising glutathione, NAC has more and better evidence than any glutathione product, at a fraction of the price.

## What about skin brightening?

Glutathione is heavily marketed for skin brightening, and this is where the gap between claims and evidence is widest. A handful of small, short trials have tested oral glutathione for skin lightening, with mixed results, and no large trial has confirmed a benefit. The claim rests on glutathione's role in melanin production, which is real biochemistry, but biochemistry is not the same as a proven cosmetic effect.

Be especially careful with injectable glutathione. The FDA has warned against unapproved injectable glutathione products marketed for skin lightening, citing safety concerns. A supplement you swallow is not the same as an injection, and the injection route carries risks no skin claim justifies.

## Who might consider it, and who should not

A liposomal glutathione or NAC supplement might be worth considering if you are older and your glutathione synthesis has slowed, if you have high oxidative stress from training, poor sleep or chronic stress, or if you simply want to support your antioxidant defenses and understand the evidence is preliminary. If that is you, NAC is the better evidenced and cheaper starting point, and a liposomal glutathione is the more expensive, less proven alternative.

Do not take glutathione or NAC without talking to a clinician first if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, if you take prescription medication, or if you have asthma. NAC in particular needs care in asthma, and it can interact with some drugs. Oral glutathione was well tolerated in trials up to six months, but long term safety beyond six months has not been studied in a controlled trial. ## Frequently asked questions

### Does oral glutathione actually work?

It can raise body stores of glutathione, but slowly and only at higher doses. A six month randomized trial found 1,000 mg a day raised glutathione 30 to 35 percent in blood cells, and 250 mg a day raised it 17 percent. A single dose does almost nothing, because gut enzymes break glutathione apart. No trial has shown oral glutathione changes a health outcome such as living longer or getting sick less often.

### Is liposomal glutathione better than regular glutathione?

Probably, for absorption, but the evidence is thin. The only published human study is a 2018 pilot in twelve adults with no placebo group, which found liposomal glutathione raised blood glutathione 40 percent in four weeks. A small 2026 crossover study also found better blood exposure from an improved delivery form. Neither study measured health outcomes, and neither tested a specific brand's product.

### Is NAC better than taking glutathione directly?

For raising glutathione levels, yes, the evidence favours NAC. NAC supplies cysteine, the rate limiting building block your body uses to make glutathione, and it reliably raises tissue glutathione in trials. It is also far cheaper, and it has a long clinical track record, including its use as the antidote for paracetamol poisoning. The best aging data is for GlyNAC, NAC combined with glycine.

### How much glutathione should you take?

There is no established dose, because no trial has tested doses against health outcomes. The trials that raised glutathione levels used 250 to 1,000 mg a day of oral glutathione for six months, or 500 to 1,000 mg a day of liposomal glutathione for four weeks. Most liposomal products, including Cymbiotika's, contain far less per serving, about 150 mg per pouch. That dose gap is worth knowing before you buy.

### Does glutathione help with skin brightening?

The evidence is weak. A handful of small, short trials have tested oral glutathione for skin lightening with mixed results, and no large trial has confirmed a benefit. Avoid injectable glutathione for skin lightening. The FDA has warned against unapproved injectable products marketed for this purpose.

### Is glutathione safe?

Oral glutathione was well tolerated in trials lasting up to six months, with no serious side effects reported. Long term safety beyond six months has not been studied in a controlled trial. NAC is generally well tolerated but can cause nausea, and it needs care in asthma and with some medications. Talk to a clinician before starting either, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or take prescription drugs.
